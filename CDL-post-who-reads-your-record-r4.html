Everyone who reads your record before you do.

Researchers, regulators, whistleblowers and the company across the street all work from the same public table. A tour of who is reading, and what they have done with it.

18 Sep 20268 min readCustoms Data Lock

In November 2021, researchers at Sheffield Hallam University published a report tracing cotton from China's Xinjiang region to Western clothing brands. Their method was not undercover work. They queried a commercial database of U.S. shipping records tier by tier, from yarn to fabric to garment, until a brand name appeared.

The researchers

That report and its 2022 sequel on the car industry, which tied more than 100 carmakers and parts makers to suppliers in the region and lists customs records as a core source, helped drive a wave of enforcement. Under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, U.S. Customs detained 16,755 shipments worth $3.69 billion between June 2022 and July 2025.

The journalism came first. In December 2018 the Associated Press traced Badger Sportswear's supply to a factory inside an internment camp using data from ImportGenius; Badger stopped sourcing there. In March 2020 the AP used ImportGenius and Panjiva records to show a Chinese touch-screen maker under scrutiny had shipped 48 cartons to the company that builds McDonald's ordering kiosks.

The competitor with a lawyer

The same records support private lawsuits. In 2016 a federal appeals court let a whistleblower case against pipe-fitting maker Victaulic go forward; the entire case had been built from a paid shipping-manifest database and a survey of eBay listings. In March 2025 a flooring importer paid $8.1 million to settle duty-evasion claims. The whistleblower was a competitor, and its share of the settlement was about $1.2 million. In December 2025 a tungsten carbide company paid $54.4 million, the largest customs settlement of its kind so far.

A competitor earned $1.2 million by reading a rival's shipping records more carefully than the rival did.Evolutions Flooring settlement, U.S. Department of Justice, March 25, 2025. The relator's counsel has said the case began with public trade data.

The executives who wanted out

In October 2022 the Associated Press reported that executives from companies including Walmart, General Motors and Intel, sitting on a Customs advisory committee, had proposed that shipping records be made private by default. Those records cover about half of the $2.7 trillion of goods the United States imports each year. Labor advocates were blunt. One said, "Corporate America should be ashamed that their answer to this abuse is to end transparency."

We think both sides of that argument are right about something. The records have exposed real abuse, and the people doing that work should keep their access. The records have also handed every importer's supplier list to its competitors, which was never the purpose.

Where we stand

Protection through Customs Data Lock changes who can read your record, not what the government knows. U.S. Customs still sees every shipment. The public does not. Regulators, courts and the agencies that enforce labor law lose nothing. The company across the street loses a free supplier list.

Who this is forLegitimate importers who want their suppliers, volumes and launches treated as the commercial information they are. If your goal is to hide from an enforcement agency, we are the wrong company to call.

We put a federal confidentiality protection in place for every entity and name you ship under, and we keep watching the platforms after that. Everyone who is entitled to read your record still can. Everyone else stops.
